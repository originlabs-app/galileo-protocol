The European Union's Digital Product Passport is no longer a proposal: its central registry went live on 20 July 2026, and the technical standards that define what a passport actually is were published on 27 May 2026. For luxury brands selling into the EU, the question has shifted from "if" to "how fast can we structure our product data" — and the brands that start now will be the ones ready when their category's delegated act lands.

## What just happened

Two milestones this summer turned the DPP from policy into infrastructure.

First, on **20 July 2026**, the European Commission opened the central DPP registry, meeting the legal deadline of 19 July 2026 set by Article 13 of the [ESPR — Regulation (EU) 2024/1781](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1781/oj). The registry is the backbone of the system: it stores passport identifiers and lets customs and market-surveillance authorities verify that a product entering the EU has a valid passport. The implementing rules governing how the registry operates took effect in early August 2026.

Second, on **27 May 2026**, CEN and CENELEC published the first six harmonised European standards for the DPP — the EN 18xxx family developed by [CEN/CLC/JTC 24](https://www.cencenelec.eu/areas-of-work/cen-cenelec-topics/ecodesign-labelling-and-traceability-of-products/labelling-and-traceability-of-products/) under Commission mandate M/604. These standards fix the technical layer: unique product identifiers, data carriers, interoperability between systems, and access rights for different stakeholders. In July 2026 they were cited in the Official Journal through [Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1736](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ:L_202601736), which gives them legal effect: a DPP built on these standards benefits from a presumption of conformity with the ESPR.

A third, quieter milestone matters for fashion and leather goods specifically: since **19 July 2026**, large enterprises are banned from destroying unsold textiles and footwear under Article 25 of the ESPR — the first product-specific ESPR obligation to bite, and a signal of where enforcement attention is heading.

## The ESPR timeline brands must plan against

The ESPR is a framework regulation: it does not impose product-level DPP duties directly, but through **delegated acts adopted per product group**. That is the mechanism to watch.

- **18 July 2024** — the ESPR entered into force. See the [European Commission's ESPR page](https://commission.europa.eu/energy-climate-change-environment/standards-tools-and-labels/products-labelling-rules-and-requirements/ecodesign-sustainable-products-regulation_en) for the framework.
- **April 2025** — the first ESPR working plan set the priorities: textiles and apparel are among the first product groups targeted.
- **27 May 2026** — first six CEN-CENELEC DPP standards published.
- **19–20 July 2026** — central DPP registry established and opened.
- **18 February 2027** — the digital battery passport becomes mandatory under the EU Battery Regulation, the first product-level passport obligation in force. It is the reference implementation every other category will be measured against.
- **~2027** — the delegated act for textiles is expected, with other product groups following the working plan's rolling calendar.

Two consequences for luxury maisons. First, watches, leather goods, jewelry and art are all in scope of the ESPR — only a handful of categories like food and medicinal products are exempt. Second, the exact date for your category is uncertain, but the shape of the obligation is not: the standards are published, the registry exists, and the battery passport will have ironed out the operational details by early 2027. Waiting for "your" delegated act to start preparing means starting 18 to 24 months late.

## What a DPP must contain

The ESPR defines the DPP as a digital identity card for products, components and materials. The precise dataset is set per product group, but the framework and the new standards converge on a common core:

- **A unique product identifier**, linked to a data carrier on the product (QR code, NFC, RFID) and registered in the central registry.
- **Product and material information**: technical performance, materials and their origin, substances of concern, recycled content.
- **Circularity data**: repairability, spare parts, disassembly and recycling instructions.
- **Compliance information**: declarations and documents demonstrating conformity with the applicable delegated act.
- **Access-controlled visibility**: consumers, repairers, recyclers, customs and market-surveillance authorities each see the slice of the passport they are entitled to — a requirement the CEN-CENELEC standards make concrete.

For luxury, this maps naturally onto what maisons already track — serial numbers, certificates, service history, provenance — but in a structured, interoperable, machine-readable form. The hard part is rarely the technology. It is the data: decades of product information scattered across PLM systems, ERP exports, atelier records and after-sales spreadsheets.

## FAQ

### Is the EU Digital Product Passport mandatory for luxury goods today?

Not yet for product-level registration. The central registry opened on 20 July 2026 as infrastructure, but product-by-product obligations start with the delegated acts per product group. The battery passport is first, from 18 February 2027; the delegated act for textiles is expected around 2027.

### What is the EU DPP registry?

The central registry required by Article 13 of the ESPR, operated by the European Commission. It stores the identifiers of Digital Product Passports and lets customs and market-surveillance authorities verify that a product has a valid passport.

### What standards define the technical format of a DPP?

The first six harmonised European standards, published by CEN and CENELEC on 27 May 2026 (the EN 18xxx family from CEN/CLC/JTC 24) and cited in the Official Journal by Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1736. They cover identifiers, data carriers, interoperability and access rights.

### Does the DPP requirement apply to watches, leather goods and jewelry?

Yes, in principle. The ESPR covers virtually all physical products placed on the EU market. The timing and data requirements for each luxury category will be set by product-specific delegated acts, with textiles among the first priorities.

### What should a luxury brand do first?

Start with data: consolidate product identifiers, bills of materials, provenance and repair records into structured, shareable data. Then align with the published CEN-CENELEC standards and run a pilot on one product line before your category's delegated act lands.
