The battery passport gives luxury brands a concrete example of a product-information obligation with a fixed start date. It does **not** give clothing or leather goods the same deadline. The useful lesson is how a legal requirement connects an identifiable product, reliable information and different readers' access rights.

Under [article 77 of Regulation (EU) 2023/1542](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1542/oj), from **18 February 2027** an electronic battery passport is required for each light means of transport battery, each industrial battery with a capacity **greater than 2 kWh**, and each electric vehicle battery placed on the market or put into service. Light means of transport include categories used in vehicles such as electric bicycles. This provision does not cover every small battery merely because it powers a luxury product.

## A product rule gives the date, not the registry launch

The [Commission's retail platform reported on 23 July 2026](https://transition-pathways.europa.eu/retail/news/digital-product-passport-registry-now-live) that the digital product passport registry had launched on 20 July. An operational registry and an applicable product obligation answer different questions. The registry supplies infrastructure; the battery regulation specifies which batteries need a passport and when.

For textile apparel, the [Commission's sector page](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/single-market/digital-product-passport/textile-apparel_en), consulted on 20 October, says the detailed requirements will be set in a future delegated act. A delegated act is a further legal text defining requirements under the wider regulation. The page's timetable is an indicative plan for adoption, not a published date on which every garment must carry a passport.

A fashion house should therefore keep two decisions separate: what it can usefully prepare now, and what an applicable rule actually requires for its products. Neither a supplier's launch date nor the battery deadline settles the second question for fashion.

## Identity needs to survive a change of hands

A battery passport contains information about the model and information about the individual battery, including information resulting from its use. Article 77 links access through a QR code to a unique identifier assigned by the operator placing the battery on the market.

Those levels matter. A model specification can explain how a product was designed. It cannot, on its own, describe what happened to a particular item after delivery. The same distinction is useful when a luxury house considers repairs or resale, even though the battery rule does not prescribe fashion's future record structure.

Consider a fictional house preparing records for a coat collection. It can distinguish the model's care instructions from a repair recorded for one customer's coat. Replacing a sleeve on that coat should not rewrite the care or material history of every coat in the collection. Whether future legal textile information must be attached at model, batch or individual level still depends on the applicable requirements. The example is a design exercise, not a forecast of that rule.

## A passport does not mean everyone sees everything

Article 77 and Annex XIII distinguish public information from information available to specified authorities and to people with a legitimate interest. Some information about the battery model is public; detailed composition and dismantling information receive different treatment, as do particular data about an individual battery.

Article 78 requires access to be free of charge according to the reader's rights. The entitlement is not unlimited access to all content. The detailed access arrangements must be read with the implementing rules referred to in article 77(9); a general explanation of the passport is not enough to decide a particular person's entitlement.

For a luxury house, this suggests a practical demonstration to ask of a provider. Let a customer, a repair professional and a member of the brand's team open the same proposed record. Check what each can read and change, and why. Public care guidance, a technical repair document and personal customer information serve different purposes. This exercise does not claim that battery access categories automatically apply to a coat.

An attractive public page is only one part of that demonstration. A professional must also be able to find the information needed for the task without receiving unrelated confidential material.

## Someone remains responsible after the scan

Article 77(4) makes the operator placing the battery on the market responsible for accurate, complete and current passport information, while allowing written authorisation for another operator to act on its behalf. Article 78 addresses availability even after the responsible operator ceases to exist or stops its EU activity. It also requires restrictions on introducing, modifying and updating data.

The regulation treats certain later events explicitly. A battery subjected to preparation for reuse, repurposing or remanufacturing receives a new passport linked to the original, with responsibility moving as specified in article 77(7). The passport ceases to exist after recycling. It is therefore misleading to promise an unchanging record that must last forever simply because it is called a passport.

Luxury businesses can learn from the need to define responsibility at each event. In the coat example, who can record a repair, what supports that entry, and what happens if the provider closes? Our [repair-record guide](/blog/2026-09-07-luxury-repairs-workshop) and [provider-exit guide](/blog/2026-09-11-dpp-provider-exit) address those operational questions without turning the battery rules into fashion obligations.

## Prepare a demonstration, not a fictional compliance date

A useful first exercise is to take one product, identify what is known about its model and its individual history, and show the record to its intended readers. Ask the provider to demonstrate an update, a controlled handover and an export of the data. Document who remains responsible when the technology or service provider changes.

The [Galileo documentation](/docs) provides background on product records. A successful demonstration can establish that a chosen workflow works with the information supplied. It cannot certify future textile compliance or authenticate a physical item by itself.

The fixed battery date helps make the distinction visible: a legal obligation has a defined product scope, responsible operators and requirements. Preparation for another category should be equally clear about which of those elements are already established and which are still awaiting the relevant legal text.
