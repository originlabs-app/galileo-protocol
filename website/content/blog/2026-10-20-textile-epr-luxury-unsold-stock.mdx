The business responsible for financing a luxury product's end of life is not necessarily the shop making its last sale. Under the EU textile framework, the answer depends on the product category and the operator first supplying it in the relevant Member State. A luxury price, a resale channel or a digital passport does not settle that question.

[Directive (EU) 2025/1892](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2025/1892/oj) introduces common textile rules into the Waste Framework Directive. Extended producer responsibility, or EPR, means that designated producers finance specified collection and waste-management activities for covered products. Member States must transpose the directive by **17 June 2027** and establish the required textile schemes by **17 April 2028**. Existing national schemes must also be considered; these EU dates are not permission to ignore obligations already applicable in a particular country.

## First check the product, not the sector label

Annex IVc identifies covered products through Combined Nomenclature codes, the EU's classification codes for goods. The list includes textile apparel under chapters 61 and 62, specified household textiles and headgear, leather clothing and accessories under heading 4203, and footwear under headings 6401 to 6405.

That is not the same as “everything a luxury house sells”. Heading [4202, which covers categories of bags and cases](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2026/2/oj), is not listed in this annex. Watches and jewellery are not listed either. A business should establish the correct classification of its actual product and check national scope rather than assume that every leather good is covered, or that everything called an accessory is excluded.

Consider a fictional house selling leather jackets, shoes and handbags. It should not place all three in a single “luxury leather” bucket. The jacket and shoe classifications can lead into Annex IVc, while a handbag classified under 4202 does not enter this particular EU list through its material alone. That distinction is about this annex, not an exemption from every waste or product rule.

## Identify the operator at the first supply in each country

The new definition in article 3(4b) covers several routes to being a producer: supplying products under one's own name or trademark, certain own-brand resales, first professional supply from another Member State or a third country, and cross-border distance sales directly to end users.

A retailer that simply sells another brand's goods is therefore not automatically the producer just because it takes the customer's payment. It may nevertheless be the producer where its role meets another part of the definition, for example by first supplying imported products in the country. There is no blanket retailer exemption.

The same definition excludes suppliers of used products assessed as fit for reuse, products derived from those used or waste products or their parts, and self-employed tailors making customised products. A resale business should retain the evidence that supports the category it relies on. Calling unsold new stock “second hand” does not establish that the exclusion applies.

Small businesses also need a careful reading. The new article 41 provision gives an application date of **17 April 2029** for enterprises with fewer than ten people and annual turnover and annual balance sheet not exceeding €2 million. It is a later application date, not a permanent exclusion. Recital 33 also explains that Member States can retain more protective measures, including an existing scheme covering microenterprises. A small label must therefore check its national position.

## What the producer finances

New article 22a lists costs including collection, transport, sorting, preparation for reuse, recycling, other recovery and disposal. It also covers information, reporting and specified research and development. Article 22c requires producers to entrust a producer responsibility organisation, an organisation that fulfils those obligations on their behalf.

Contributions are based on weight and, where appropriate, quantity, with adjustments under the relevant environmental criteria. The directive does not publish a single EU luxury fee that can be calculated from retail price. Nor does it promise a reduced contribution simply because a product has a passport.

Registration is a separate task. Under article 22b, producers register in each Member State where they first make the covered products available. A national producer registration identifies a responsible business. It is not the same record as a product passport, which concerns a product and its information.

For the fictional house, useful preparation is a country-by-country account of the responsible legal entity, product classification, first supply, quantities and weight. Reconcile that information with the relevant national scheme and the appointed organisation before treating a general policy summary as a fee instruction.

## Unsold stock raises a different question

Unsold goods are not automatically waste. Their status depends on what happens to them. The directive specifically addresses discarded unsold textile products: under new article 22d(2), fractions of textile materials and items must be kept separate at the point of waste generation where that separation facilitates reuse, preparation for reuse or recycling, under the conditions in that provision.

For covered goods entering the collection system, article 22a(10) and (11) also distinguish which first-market dates are relevant depending on whether a national EPR scheme already existed. The fact that a collection happens after 2028 does not, by itself, resolve how every historical batch is treated.

Paying for waste management does not grant permission to destroy stock. Our [guide to the ESPR destruction ban and disclosure duties](/blog/2026-08-29-espr-destruction-ban-luxury-disclosure) covers that separate regime. A decision to sell, donate, discard or treat goods must be described accurately before the business assigns it to a reporting category.

In the fictional house, an unsold jacket offered through an outlet and a damaged jacket discarded into a waste stream need different records. The outlet route should not automatically be reported as waste preparation for reuse. The discarded route needs its own classification, destination and supporting documents. A resale listing alone does not establish the treatment that actually took place.

## Use product records to connect the decisions

A product passport can help connect a model or batch to its material information and later events. It cannot choose the producer under national law or replace registration and scheme reporting.

A practical working file can link the product classification, first-supply record, responsible entity, relevant scheme and evidence of the final route. Keep the distinction between a product still being sold and a product discarded as waste visible. The [Galileo documentation](/docs) explains product-record concepts; the legal classification comes first.

The next useful decision is to take one product family in one country and agree which company is responsible, which scheme applies and what evidence already exists. That gives finance, logistics and product teams a shared factual starting point without assuming that every luxury category, every country or every second-hand transaction follows the same rule.
