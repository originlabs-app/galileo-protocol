An authentic handbag can still be stolen. An assessment of its materials, construction or brand identifiers answers a different question from whether the seller is entitled to sell it. A resale business needs both checks before it describes an item as ready for sale.

A [French government release published on 20 October 2026](https://presse.economie.gouv.fr/vente-de-produits-voles-lors-des-pillages-le-gouvernement-mobilise-les-plateformes/) says stolen goods were identified on online marketplaces following recent looting. It reports a meeting with platforms on 19 October and commitments to stronger monitoring, cooperation with investigators and reporting. The release does not identify luxury brands, individual sellers or the number of luxury items involved. It supports a warning about stolen goods entering resale, not an accusation against a particular handbag listing.

For a luxury reseller, the useful question is practical: what evidence connects this seller, this object and a legitimate route of acquisition?

## Treat a warning sign as a reason to investigate

The government identifies unusually low prices, batches of new products, attached security tags and explicit references to theft as signals for closer monitoring. It also says vigilance should cover requests to buy, not only offers to sell.

These signals help decide what needs attention. A low price alone does not establish theft, and an ordinary-looking listing does not establish lawful origin. A buyer should not turn a suspicion into a public accusation. A reseller should avoid turning a favourable authenticity result into approval of every other aspect of the transaction.

Consider a fictional dealer offered several unused bags by someone who says they came from a boutique clearance. The bags pass a specialist authenticity assessment. The seller supplies a screenshot of a receipt, but it does not identify the batch offered or explain how the seller acquired it. The open question is the acquisition trail. Paying for another authenticity opinion would not answer it.

## Build an acquisition trail that can be checked

For that dealer, we recommend reviewing three connections before accepting the consignment. This is a practical intake method, not a promise that a checklist establishes legal ownership.

- **The person and the sale.** Establish who is offering the goods and whether they claim to own them or act for someone else. Where someone acts for an owner, ask for evidence of that authority. A platform account name alone does not explain that relationship.
- **The document and the object.** Compare the invoice or transfer record with the item description, date, quantity and any relevant identifiers. An invoice for one bag does not explain a batch of ten. Record mismatches rather than silently assuming that similar descriptions refer to the same goods.
- **The document and its source.** Where confirmation is needed, contact the named retailer, auctioneer or other issuer through independently obtained contact details. Ask whether it can verify the relevant document and item details. An inability to obtain confirmation remains an unanswered question, not proof of theft or proof that the sale is safe.

Not every legitimate owner retains an original retail invoice. A gift, inheritance or earlier resale may involve different records. The dealer should ask for an explanation and supporting evidence appropriate to that route, rather than treating one missing document as a verdict. Conversely, a document headed “invoice” does not resolve contradictory information by itself.

In the fictional clearance example, the next request is evidence of the supplier sale and how its contents correspond to the bags offered. Until the material discrepancy is resolved, we recommend pausing acceptance and publication of the listing. Keep the reason for that pause separate from the specialist's assessment that the bags appear genuine.

## French law makes the knowledge element important

[Article 321-1 of the French Criminal Code](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/id/LEGISCTA000006149837) defines *recel* through knowingly concealing, holding or passing on property derived from a crime or offence, acting as an intermediary for its transfer, or knowingly benefiting from its proceeds. The fact that an item is authentic does not answer that question. Equally, the provision's knowledge requirement should not be replaced with a claim that every buyer of stolen goods is automatically guilty.

The same chapter contains a distinct record-keeping rule. Article 321-7 concerns professionals selling used movable goods or goods acquired from people other than their manufacturers or traders. It requires a daily register identifying the goods, their provenance, payment method and the people supplying them, under conditions set by decree. A French professional reseller should check the applicable register requirements; the suggested intake file above is not a substitute for that statutory register.

These are French provisions. They do not determine ownership disputes, recovery rights or criminal liability in every resale market. Where an actual theft allegation concerns an item, obtain advice appropriate to the jurisdiction and follow the platform's reporting process and the competent authorities' instructions.

## Preserve the evidence without publishing an accusation

If the seller's explanation conflicts with the records or a credible theft report concerns the item, retain the listing reference, relevant messages, document versions and the reason for the concern in a restricted case file. Do not publish the seller's personal documents in a product listing or public passport.

For the fictional dealer, a useful internal note would say: “Acquisition evidence does not yet connect the supplier sale to the batch offered.” It should not say “counterfeit” when the unresolved issue is origin, or “stolen” when that has not been established. This wording helps the next reviewer understand what evidence is missing and what could resolve it.

The government release describes platform cooperation with investigators. That does not make an ordinary buyer an investigator or authorise a reseller to make its own determination of guilt. A report should distinguish what was observed from what is suspected.

## A passport can carry evidence, not create a right to sell

A digital product passport can link an item to records about its origin and later events. A record is useful only to the extent that the relevant statement, its source and its connection to the physical item can be checked. Possession of a code or control of a digital token does not, by itself, establish the seller's legal authority over the object.

For this intake decision, a useful product record would point to the acquisition documents and who supplied them, while keeping private evidence behind appropriate access controls. It should make an unresolved origin check visible to the authorised reviewer instead of converting it into an unqualified approval for the buyer.
