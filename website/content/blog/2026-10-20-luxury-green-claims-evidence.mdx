A luxury label that calls a product “sustainable” or “conscious” needs more than a link to a convincing-looking passport. The shopper must understand what the claim concerns, and the business must be able to support it. Evidence about one material does not automatically justify a promise about the whole product.

The new EU consumer-law measures have an application date of **27 September 2026**. They come from [Directive (EU) 2024/825](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/825/oj), which required Member States to transpose its provisions by 27 March 2026 and apply those national measures from September. A directive works through national law: the seller must check the implementing rules and enforcement arrangements in its markets. This article explains the EU framework, not a country-by-country finding that every national implementation is identical.

## Start with the promise the shopper actually sees

The directive adds a prohibition on generic environmental claims where the trader cannot demonstrate recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to that claim. This is a defined legal standard, including the EU Ecolabel and officially recognised Type I ecolabelling schemes, rather than any favourable supplier document a brand happens to hold.

Recital 10 makes a further distinction for words such as “conscious”, “sustainable” and “responsible”. They can communicate social characteristics as well as environmental ones. Recognised environmental performance alone is therefore not enough to support that broader promise. The text does not establish a simple list of words that are always safe or always forbidden regardless of context.

Look at the complete presentation: the words, nearby explanation, imagery and any badge. The directive separately addresses sustainability labels, which must be based on a qualifying certification scheme or established by public authorities. Designing a green badge in-house does not turn a broad marketing promise into independent certification.

For a brand, the practical question is what its evidence actually establishes. For a shopper, it is what the label gives them reason to believe. Those two answers should match.

## A specific statement belongs beside the claim

A claim is not treated as generic where its specification is clear and prominent on the same medium. The [Commission's questions and answers, published on 22 September 2026](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/3c257883-bb2a-4dd9-a6dc-501d587bb34f_en?filename=Q%26A%20ECGT_Directive_Sept%202026.pdf), explain this distinction in question 4. A specific statement still has to satisfy the other rules against misleading practices; specificity is not an automatic approval.

Consider a fictional fashion house that has evidence about the recycled fibres in a coat's lining. Calling the entire coat “sustainable” would communicate much more than that evidence establishes. A narrower statement would identify the lining, the material concerned and the verified proportion. The brand would still need to substantiate that statement and check that the surrounding presentation does not imply the same result for the shell, buttons or whole garment.

The directive also prohibits presenting an environmental benefit of one aspect as a benefit of the entire product or business. That is why moving the precise wording from a product page into a distant document can change what a customer understands.

A QR code can lead to supporting documents. It should not be treated as a substitute for the clear, prominent specification required alongside a claim on a label or other medium. A customer should not have to open a separate evidence file to discover that “recycled” referred only to the packaging.

## Give the passport a supporting job

A digital product passport is a product-linked record reached through a code or another identifier. It can help a customer or professional find information about the item. It does not itself establish that a statement is true, independently verified or legally permitted.

For this editorial example, a useful evidence record would connect:

- the exact wording displayed, including the component to which it refers;
- the product model or batch covered by the evidence;
- the source, method, date and limits of the supporting document;
- the person responsible for checking whether the statement remains accurate.

This is a proposed working record, not a new list of statutory passport fields. If a supplier document applies to one production batch, the record should not silently extend it to every later collection. If the wording changes, keep enough history to understand which evidence supported the version a customer saw.

The [Galileo documentation](/docs) introduces product-record concepts. The important design choice here is the relationship between claim and evidence, rather than a particular storage technology. ## Existing stock still needs a decision

A previous season's label does not receive a blanket exemption simply because it was printed before September. Question 18 of the Commission's guidance addresses existing products and packaging. It describes practical corrections, such as covering or correcting wording and providing appropriate information near affected products at the point of sale.

The Commission's [summary of the consumer authorities' common understanding on old stock](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/consumers/consumer-rights-and-complaints/enforcement-consumer-protection/coordinated-actions/sustainable-consumption-actions_en) also describes a proportionate, case-by-case enforcement approach. Authorities may consider genuine transitional difficulties and reasonable corrective efforts. This is not a universal extension of the application date or a guarantee that an unchanged label will be accepted.

For the fictional coat, the next step is to review the online listing and physical label together, identify what needs correcting, and document the action under the relevant national rules. Printing another code is not the first decision. Agreeing on a claim that says exactly what the evidence supports is.

## Sources and scope

The legal basis is Directive (EU) 2024/825, particularly its definitions, recitals 7 to 12, amendments to the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and article 4.
